Flip a switch and see who gets the gains from AI

A small economy: 100 people and four AI firms doing R&D for 25 periods. The four institutions come from two choices: whether research results are open, and who pays for and owns the firms. Start with the overview to compare the four; then flip the assumptions on the left to see which conclusions change; then scroll down to watch how any one institution plays out.

TL;DRthe findings in five sentences

  • Open research needs someone to pay for it. With public funding, open research ties for the highest technology and gives the largest gains to workers and others (under the default assumptions).
  • Privately funded open research does not hold up. When firms choose their own R&D, open results are shared by everyone, so a firm gets little back from its own spending and hardly invests; when openness also erodes pricing power, firms lose money and exit.
  • Private closed research drifts toward monopoly. When pricing depends on the lead, laggards exit and usually one firm is left; technology is even the highest, but the gains go mostly to investors and workers get the least.
  • How large the open advantage is depends on how well open results can be used. The default is the most favorable case: open results are absorbed in full, firms’ research paths are uncorrelated, and progress never gets harder, so open research always reaches about 10. Relaxing these assumptions with the “Frictions in technical progress” switch shrinks the advantage. Under public funding open research rarely does worse than closed; under private funding it can.
  • A public lab need not crowd out private R&D. When private firms are closed, an open public lab makes them invest more; when private firms are open too, it replaces their R&D entirely. Its results are mostly monetized by private owners.
